How to: Stay Safe When Your AI Agent Reads the Web

Last reviewed: October 13, 2026Reading time: about 9 minutesBy Ronak

AI agents can now browse websites, read your email, and click buttons for you. That is useful. It also means anything the agent reads can try to give it orders. This guide explains how that happens, why the usual fixes keep falling short, and what you can check before you switch an agent on.

This is an independent student guide. It is not affiliated with EFF, Consumer Reports, or any AI company. Attacks described here were patched or tested in research settings; the date above tells you how current the advice is.

The short version

One researcher, one trick, two very different results

In October 2023, security researcher Johann Rehberger started a ChatGPT conversation with a few lines of private chat. Then he uploaded a cartoon. The cartoon had speech bubbles, and the speech bubbles held instructions. ChatGPT, which could now "see" images, read the bubbles and obeyed them. It packed a summary of the private conversation into a web link pointing at Rehberger's own server. When the app tried to display that link as an image, the summary landed in his server log (Willison, 2023).

A year later, Rehberger tried the same idea on an agent that could control a whole computer: Anthropic's Claude Computer Use, then in beta. This time there was no cartoon. He made a web page with one sentence on it and asked the agent to visit it (Rehberger, 2024).

The trick didn't change. What changed was what the AI was allowed to do. In 2023 it could leak one conversation. In 2024 it could hand over the whole machine. That is the main idea of this guide, and the rest of the page builds on it.

What's going on

Your agent can't tell your words from the page's words

Both attacks are a kind of .Prompt injection: text or images that an AI reads while doing a task, written so the AI treats them as new instructions. The attacker never talks to the AI directly. They plant the instructions where the AI will look. When you ask an agent to do something, it reads your request and everything on the screen as one long stream. There is no hard wall between "what my owner asked" and "what this website says." A group of security researchers put it simply: these AI-powered apps blur the line between data and instructions (Greshake et al., 2023).

If this sounds familiar, it should. It is phishing, aimed at your agent instead of at you. Phishing guides teach people not to trust instructions that show up in their inbox (EFF, 2024). An agent has no such instinct. Simon Willison, who has tracked these attacks since 2022, says it plainly:

"Large Language Models are gullible."Simon Willison, writing about the speech-bubble attack (2023)

He adds that we need them to stay that way, since following instructions is the whole point of an assistant. That is why the problem is hard.

Images make it harder. An instruction can be printed in a cartoon, hidden in a banner ad, or drawn as a fake pop-up. Text filters that scan web pages for suspicious words never see it, because to them it is just a picture.

One more term worth knowing, because people mix them up: .Jailbreaking: tricking an AI into breaking its own safety rules, like getting it to explain something dangerous. The person doing it is usually the user. Prompt injection is different: a third party sneaks orders into content the AI reads, and the victim is the user (Willison, 2024). This guide is about injection, where you are the victim, not the one doing the tricking.

How often it works

In tests, agents fall for it a lot

Researchers have built test setups to measure this. The numbers below come from two of them. They are lab tests where the attacker knew roughly what the agent was doing, so read them as "how bad it can get," not "what happens every day."

The pop-up study also tried the obvious fix: telling the agent to ignore pop-ups. It didn't work (Zhang et al., 2025). The other study found that adding safety warnings to the agent's instructions gave only small improvements (Cao et al., 2026). The industry agrees this is serious. OWASP, a nonprofit that ranks software security risks, lists prompt injection as the number-one risk for AI apps and specifically warns about instructions hidden in images (OWASP, 2025).

Why "safer models" aren't enough

Teaching the AI to say no is a promise, not a lock

AI companies train their models to spot and refuse malicious instructions. That helps, and it is getting better. Anthropic reports that its browser agent, after new training and filters, was fooled about 1% of the time by its own strongest automated attacker (Anthropic, 2025). That is a big improvement. The company is also upfront about its limits:

"We share these findings to demonstrate progress, not to claim the problem is solved."Anthropic, on its browser agent's prompt injection defenses (2025)

Images add a specific weak spot. One study rewrote forbidden requests as pictures of text and got AI models to go along with them about 82% of the time on average. The authors traced this to safety training that mostly covered words and not images (Gong et al., 2025). That study was about jailbreaking, not injection, but it shows the gap: rules learned from text don't carry over cleanly to pictures.

And 1% sounds small until you think about volume. An agent that reads hundreds of pages a week will eventually meet one that was built to trick it. As Anthropic puts it, every webpage an agent visits is a potential vector for attack (Anthropic, 2025).

What actually helps

Limits beat good intentions

Your password manager is a good model here. A fake login page can fool a person, but it can't fool a password manager, because the manager checks the web address, not how the page looks (EFF, 2024). It doesn't need good judgment. It just can't do the wrong thing.

Security researchers have started designing agents this way on purpose. One group laid out design patterns that keep an agent from acting on untrusted content, and was clear that each pattern trades away some usefulness for safety (Beurer-Kellner et al., 2025). Another team built a computer-control agent that writes its whole plan before it looks at any web page, so a page can't add new steps. It kept up to 57% of a top agent's task performance (Foerster et al., 2026).

That design still isn't perfect. The same team found that a manipulated screen can still push the agent down the wrong path within its plan, like sending it to a bad website (Foerster et al., 2026). Limits don't make an agent invincible. They make the worst case smaller, and that is what you can control as a user.

Before you turn it on

Questions to ask yourself

The best advice would be "don't give agents access to anything important." That isn't realistic if you want them to be useful. So instead, judge an agent by what it can't do. Look in its settings and help pages for answers to these.

  • Does it ask me before it sends, buys, posts, or deletes anything?
    This is the single most important limit. A tricked agent that has to ask first gives you a chance to notice.
    All agents
  • Can it download files and run them?
    This is exactly what made the 2024 demo so bad. If the answer is yes, keep it away from your main computer.
    Computer-control agents, browsers
  • Does it load images or links from any website automatically?
    That's how the 2023 attack leaked a private chat. Apps that only show images from trusted places close that door.
    Email AI, browsers
  • Which of my logged-in accounts can it reach?
    An agent in your everyday browser acts as you: your email, your bank, your work tools. A separate browser profile with fewer logins limits the damage.
    All agents
  • Can I watch what it's doing and stop it?
    Supervise it on sensitive sites. If it starts doing something you didn't ask for, that is your signal.
    Computer-control agents, browsers
  • Can it forward or reply to email on its own?
    Anyone can send you an email, which means anyone can send your agent instructions. Drafts you approve are safer than automatic sending.
    Email AI

A few habits that help right now

  • Don't use agents for banking, taxes, or anything you can't undo.
  • Give the agent only the task it needs, on the sites it needs.
